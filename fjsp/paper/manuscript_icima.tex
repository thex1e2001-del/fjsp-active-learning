\begin{document}\maketitle\begin{abstract}
Flexible Job Shop Scheduling (FJSP) couples routing and sequencing decisions, making repeated exact evaluation expensive. This paper proposes an active-learning-assisted candidate-screening workflow for intelligent manufacturing. A low-cost heuristic first ranks assignment candidates; a small, deliberately selected calibration set is then verified by a fixed-assignment MIP, and the resulting incumbent makespans train a Random Forest (AL-RF) to rank the remaining candidates. All final scorers use the same independent test pool, Top-$K$ verification budget, and solver time limit. Across Brandimarte MK01--MK10, with two deterministic seeds per instance and a five-second verification budget, AL-RF achieves the lowest mean best feasible makespan among the tested screeners and improves over the original RF-H, lower-bound, direct-heuristic, and random controls. The released protocol records candidate identities, solver status, feasibility, optimality gaps, and calibration cost, allowing the gain and its computational trade-off to be audited.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} flexible job shop scheduling; random forest; surrogate model; decomposition; heuristic labeling; interpretable screening
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern manufacturing environments increasingly require flexible production systems where jobs can be routed through alternative machines depending on shop-floor conditions. This flexibility, while valuable for throughput and resilience, introduces a challenging combinatorial problem: the Flexible Job Shop Scheduling Problem (FJSP). Given $n$ jobs each comprising a sequence of operations and $m$ machines with machine-dependent processing times, the scheduler must simultaneously decide \emph{which} machine processes each operation (routing) and \emph{in what order} operations are processed on each machine (sequencing), to minimize the maximum completion time (makespan).

The coupling of routing and sequencing is the central computational difficulty of FJSP. The routing decision determines which operations compete for each machine, while the sequencing decision resolves those competitions. Exact mixed-integer programming (MIP) formulations must jointly optimize both, leading to a search space that grows as $O(m^{N} \times N!)$ for $N$ operations. While modern solvers such as Gurobi can prove optimality on small instances (e.g., 55 operations), they fail to close the optimality gap within reasonable time on larger instances with 150--240 operations \cite{Ozguven2010}. Meta-heuristics, most notably the tabu search algorithm of \cite{Mastrolilli2000}, can find near-optimal solutions through carefully designed neighborhood structures, but they rely on hand-crafted operators, require instance-specific tuning, and do not learn from data to improve over time.

Recent advances in machine learning for combinatorial optimization have opened new avenues \cite{Bengio2021}. End-to-end approaches using deep reinforcement learning or graph neural networks can learn effective dispatching policies \cite{Zhang2020drl,Song2023} but require extensive training data and offer limited interpretability. Tree-in-MIP methods embed learned decision trees or random forests as MIP constraints \cite{Maragno2023}, but the standard per-data-point encoding creates $O(N \times T \times L)$ binary variables---prohibitive for FJSP where each operation is a separate data point. Surrogate-assisted optimization \cite{Jin2011} offers a middle ground: use a cheap predictive model to guide search, reserving expensive exact evaluation for promising candidates only.

This paper investigates whether lightweight, interpretable features of a machine assignment can provide useful guidance for screening candidate assignments under a fixed verification budget. A machine assignment is represented by workload, operation-count, machine-usage, imbalance, dispersion, and maximum job-time features. A fast heuristic sequencer generates low-cost proxy labels, a Random Forest surrogate scores unseen assignments, and a time-limited fixed-assignment MIP verifies the same number of candidates for every scorer.

Our research is guided by four questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Can aggregate assignment features support effective ranking of candidate assignments, as measured by top-$K$ recall and regret metrics?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Under fair computational budgets, does a learned RF surrogate add value over simple workload-based scoring rules?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Under what instance structures does the method succeed, and under what conditions does it fail?
    \item[\textbf{RQ4.}] What limitations become visible when solver status, feasibility, and optimality gaps are reported at candidate level?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item An active-learning screening architecture that uses a small MIP-verified calibration set to correct the systematic bias of heuristic sequencing labels before ranking unseen machine assignments.
\item A budget-matched evaluation protocol with independent training and test pools, fixed Top-$K$ verification, and candidate-level solver evidence.
\item A two-seed-per-instance Brandimarte evaluation showing that AL-RF reduces the mean best feasible makespan relative to RF-H, lower-bound, direct heuristic, and random screeners under the same final verification budget, while making calibration overhead explicit.
\end{itemize}

The paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews related work and positions the workflow. Section~3 formalizes the problem and presents lower-bound motivation. Section~4 describes the methodology. Section~5 presents computational experiments. Section~6 discusses findings, limitations, and manufacturing implications. Section~7 concludes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{FJSP: Problem, Benchmarks, and Foundations}
FJSP was formalized by \cite{Brucker1990} as a generalization of the classical job shop. The standard benchmark suites---Brandimarte \cite{Brandimarte1993}, Hurink \textit{edata}/\textit{rdata}/\textit{vdata} \cite{Hurink1994}, Dauz\`ere-P\'er\`es and Paulli \cite{Dauzere1997}, Kacem \cite{Kacem2002}, Fattahi \cite{Fattahi2007}, and others---have driven algorithm development for three decades. FJSPLib provides a centralized repository of instances and best-known lower/upper bounds across these suites. \cite{Pinedo2016} provides a comprehensive textbook treatment. \cite{Xie2019} classify FJSP solution methods into exact algorithms, heuristics, and meta-heuristics.

\subsection{Exact, MIP, and CP Methods}
Exact MIP formulations jointly model assignment and sequencing \cite{Ozguven2010,Meng2020}. While they can prove optimality on small instances, their computational cost grows exponentially. Constraint Programming (CP) approaches, notably CP-SAT and IBM CP Optimizer, have demonstrated strong performance on FJSP benchmarks through interval variables and specialized scheduling constraints. However, both MIP and CP solvers still require substantial time on medium-to-large instances, motivating decomposition and heuristic approaches.

\subsection{Metaheuristics and Hybrid Methods}
The tabu search of \cite{Mastrolilli2000} remains a landmark, achieving near-optimal makespan through hierarchical neighborhood structures. Subsequent work includes genetic algorithms \cite{Pezzella2008}, hybrid genetic-tabu search \cite{Li2016}, variable neighborhood search \cite{Gao2008}, and particle swarm optimization hybrids \cite{Xia2005}. \cite{Calis2015} review AI techniques for job shop scheduling. These methods are effective but hand-crafted: their operators, neighborhoods, and parameters are designed for specific benchmark characteristics and do not generalize without re-engineering.

\subsection{Decomposition in Scheduling}
Decomposition has a long history in scheduling. The shifting bottleneck procedure \cite{Adams1988} iteratively solves single-machine subproblems. For FJSP, several works separate assignment and sequencing: \cite{Brandimarte1993} uses a hierarchical tabu search, and \cite{Xia2005} hybridize particle swarm optimization for assignment with simulated annealing for sequencing. These methods use fixed heuristics for the assignment subproblem. Our work differs by \emph{learning} an evaluation function for assignments from data.

\subsection{Learning-Based Scheduling}
The intersection of machine learning and scheduling has grown rapidly. End-to-end methods use deep reinforcement learning to learn dispatching policies: \cite{Zhang2020drl} represent job shop states as disjunctive graphs and train a GNN-based agent; \cite{Song2023} extend this to FJSP with heterogeneous graph representations for joint operation selection and machine assignment; \cite{Wang2024} propose dual attention networks for FJSP; and \cite{Moon2024learning} address FJSP with transportation constraints. \cite{Smit2024nco} extend neural combinatorial optimization to stochastic FJSP. These methods can achieve strong empirical performance but require extensive training and are difficult to interpret.

Learning-to-configure approaches use ML to accelerate existing solvers: \cite{Hutter2011} for algorithm configuration, \cite{He2014} and \cite{Khalil2016} for branching in MIP, and \cite{Gasse2019} for node selection in B\&B via graph convolutional networks. \cite{Kotary2021} survey end-to-end constrained optimization learning.

\subsection{Surrogate-Assisted Combinatorial Optimization}
Surrogate models replace expensive objective evaluations with cheap approximations. \cite{Jin2011} surveys surrogate-assisted evolutionary computation, primarily for continuous optimization. Extensions to discrete and combinatorial problems are more recent. The key challenge in discrete settings is defining feature representations that preserve the ranking of decision alternatives under the true objective. Our work addresses this by designing structured aggregate features for the FJSP assignment space and validating ranking quality through top-$K$ metrics.

\subsection{Tree-in-MIP and Constraint Learning}
\cite{Verwer2017} first proposed encoding decision trees as MIP constraints. \cite{Maragno2023} generalized this into OptiCL, a framework embedding decision trees, random forests, ensembles, and neural networks into MIP formulations. \cite{Elmachtoub2022} formalize the ``predict, then optimize'' paradigm. \cite{Bertsimas2017} develop optimal classification trees via MIP. The fundamental limitation for FJSP is the per-data-point encoding: $O(N \times T \times L)$ binary variables for $N$ operations, $T$ trees, and $L$ leaves. Our aggregate feature approach avoids this by using globally shared feature variables, reducing encoding complexity to $O(T \times L)$.

\subsection{Positioning}
The workflow positions itself between lightweight heuristics and learning-assisted exact verification. Our framework combines (1)~lower-bound-motivated assignment features, (2)~inexpensive heuristic labeling, (3)~a learned but interpretable surrogate, and (4)~MIP-based verification with explicit reporting of limitations.

\section{Problem Formulation and Lower-Bound Motivation}

\subsection{Notation}
\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\caption{Notation.}\label{tab:notation}
\begin{tabular}{ll}\toprule
$J=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ & Set of jobs \\
$M=\{1,\ldots,m\}$ & Set of machines \\
$\mathcal{O}=\{(j,p)\}$ & Set of all operations \\
$M_{j,p}\subseteq M$ & Feasible machines for $(j,p)$ \\
$p_{j,p,k}$ & Processing time of $(j,p)$ on $k$ \\
$x_{j,p,k}\in\{0,1\}$ & Assignment variable \\
$S_{j,p},C_{j,p}\geq 0$ & Start and completion times \\
$\delta_{(j,p),(j',p'),k}\in\{0,1\}$ & Sequencing variable \\
$C_{\max}$ & Makespan \\
$H$ & Big-M constant ($\sum_{j,p,k}p_{j,p,k}$) \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Full FJSP MIP Formulation}
The standard MIP minimizes $C_{\max}$ subject to:
\begin{align}
\sum_{k\in M_{j,p}} x_{j,p,k} &= 1 \quad \forall (j,p)\in\mathcal{O} \label{eq:c1}\\
C_{j,p} &= S_{j,p} + \sum_{k\in M_{j,p}} p_{j,p,k}\,x_{j,p,k} \quad \forall (j,p) \label{eq:c2}\\
S_{j,p+1} &\geq C_{j,p} \quad \forall j, p< o_j \label{eq:c3}\\
S_{j',p'} &\geq C_{j,p} - H(1-\delta) - H(2-x_{j,p,k}-x_{j',p',k}) \label{eq:c4}\\
C_{\max} &\geq C_{j,p} \quad \forall (j,p) \label{eq:c5}
\end{align}
where~\eqref{eq:c4} is enforced for all machine-sharing operation pairs with the complementary constraint $S_{j,p} \geq C_{j',p'} - H\delta - H(2-x_{j,p,k}-x_{j',p',k})$.

\subsection{Lower Bounds and Feature Motivation}
Given a fixed assignment $\mathbf{x}$, the sequencing subproblem is:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{SEQ}(\mathbf{x}):\; \min_{\delta,S,C}\, C_{\max}\;\text{ s.t. }\;\eqref{eq:c2}--\eqref{eq:c5}\text{ with }\mathbf{x}\text{ fixed}. \label{eq:seq}
\end{equation}

Two classical lower bounds motivate our feature design:

\begin{description}
\item[Proposition 1 (Machine workload bound).] For any feasible schedule under assignment $\mathbf{x}$: 
$C_{\max} \geq \max_{k\in M} \sum_{(j,p):x_{j,p,k}=1} p_{j,p,k}$.
\item[Proposition 2 (Job path bound).] For any feasible schedule: 
$C_{\max} \geq \max_{j\in J} \sum_{p} \sum_{k} p_{j,p,k}\,x_{j,p,k}$.
\end{description}

\textit{Proof.} Proposition~1 holds because $\max_k \operatorname{load}_k$ is the earliest possible completion time of the bottleneck machine. Proposition~2 holds because each job's operations must be processed sequentially, so total job time is a lower bound on its completion time. $\square$

These bounds justify machine workload and maximum job-time features as surrogate inputs. They are necessary but not sufficient: two assignments with similar aggregate loads can still have different sequencing difficulty because of precedence and machine interactions. The experiments therefore treat the lower bound as a baseline scorer and do not claim that the implemented feature vector captures the full fixed-assignment objective.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Aggregate Feature Representation}
We represent an assignment $\mathbf{x}$ by the workload, operation-count, usage, path-time, and dispersion features implemented in the released experiment code and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:features}. All features are computable from $\mathbf{x}$ alone and do not require solving the sequencing subproblem.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\caption{Feature groups implemented in the released scorer.}\label{tab:features}
\scriptsize\begin{tabular}{p{1.15cm}p{2.35cm}p{4.0cm}}\toprule
Group & Features & Motivation \\ \midrule
\textbf{A. Machine assignment} & $\operatorname{load}_k$, $\operatorname{ops}_k$, $\operatorname{used}_k$ for each machine & Represent machine workload and assignment sparsity \\
\textbf{B. Aggregate workload} & number of used machines, maximum machine load, load imbalance, maximum operations per machine, load standard deviation & Lower-bound and balance indicators \\
\textbf{C. Job-path summary} & maximum total processing time of any job under the assignment & Proposition~2: critical job-path bound \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Heuristic Sequencer and Labeling Procedure}
We design a fast constructive heuristic sequencer to generate proxy labels $\tilde{C}_{\max}(\mathbf{x})$ for training. The sequencer maintains the ready set of operations whose predecessors have completed, applies the implemented dispatching rules, and returns the best resulting makespan with deterministic tie-breaking. The implementation performs no backtracking or insertion; its runtime is measured empirically rather than assigned an overly specific asymptotic bound.

Training and test pools are generated using different deterministic seeds and deduplicated within each pool. Labels are fitted from the training pool only. The preserved formal runs were audited for zero cross-pool assignment intersection, and the current runner enforces the same invariant before scoring.

\subsection{Active-Learning Surrogate}
The initial RF-H model is trained on the cheap heuristic labels. We then select $q$ training assignments from the low-heuristic-score region, solve their fixed-assignment sequencing problems with the same MIP model, and fit a second Random Forest directly to the feasible incumbent makespans. This active model, denoted AL-RF, is trained only on calibration assignments and is used to score the independent test pool. The calibration set is deliberately concentrated near the predicted frontier, where ranking errors matter most. If fewer than five feasible labels are returned, AL-RF falls back to RF-H and records the event.

\subsection{Budget-Matched Candidate Screening}
The final comparison uses a common test pool. LB, RF-H, AL-RF, HeurSeq, and Random score exactly the same candidates and each submit $K$ candidates to the fixed-assignment MIP. AL-RF has an explicit calibration cost of $q$ MIP calls; this cost is reported separately from the final screening budget. All methods receive the same final $K$ verifications, so the comparison answers whether active calibration improves the quality of the final shortlist.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]\caption{Active-Learning Candidate Screening}\label{alg:beam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Instance, training pool $\mathcal{T}$, test pool $\mathcal{V}$, calibration size $q$, top-$K$
\State Generate heuristic labels on $\mathcal{T}$ and fit the initial RF-H model
\State Select $q$ low-heuristic-score assignments from $\mathcal{T}$ and verify them with the fixed-assignment MIP
\State Fit AL-RF on the feasible MIP incumbents; fall back to RF-H if fewer than five labels are available
\For{each scorer $s\in\{LB, RF\mbox{-}H, AL\mbox{-}RF, HeurSeq, Random\}$}
    \State Score the common test pool $\mathcal{V}$
    \State Select the same number $K$ of lowest-scoring assignments
    \State Verify selected assignments with the identical final MIP budget
\EndFor
\State Report incumbent, bound, status, gap, feasibility, calibration calls, and wall time
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{MIP-Based Sequencing Verification}
Top-$K$ candidates and calibration assignments are verified by solving $\operatorname{SEQ}(\mathbf{x})$ with Gurobi 10.0 and a fixed five-second time limit. We record incumbent makespan, best bound, solver status, MIP gap, and wall time. A time-limited feasible incumbent is not treated as a proof of optimality; all reported improvements therefore refer to feasible incumbent quality under the declared budget.

\textit{Implementation.} The rerun used Gurobi 10.0 on an Intel Core Ultra 5 125H (16 GB RAM) with scikit-learn RF models (100 trees, depth 12, minimum leaf size 5). For each instance and seed, the protocol generated 200 training assignments and 100 independent test assignments, requested 30 calibration labels, selected $K=5$, and gave every calibration and final selected assignment a five-second MIP verification budget. The active-learning development protocol was evaluated with seeds 42 and 43. The complete candidate-level output is released as JSON; earlier single-seed beam-search files are retained only as historical exploratory context.

\textit{Baselines.} We compare scorers under a common candidate pool and verification budget:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{LB}: $\max\{\max_k\operatorname{load}_k,\max_j\operatorname{jobTime}_j\}$.
\item \textbf{RF-H}: RF trained only on heuristic sequencing labels from the training pool.
\item \textbf{AL-RF}: RF trained on the small MIP-verified calibration set described above.
\item \textbf{HeurSeq} and \textbf{Random}: direct heuristic and seeded random rankings of the same test pool.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Metrics.} For each instance-method pair, we report the best selected feasible incumbent, mean and standard deviation across seeds, feasible ratio, low-gap ratio, mean feasible gap, and end-to-end wall time. Because several large-instance candidates have no feasible incumbent within five seconds, missing values are retained rather than imputed. BKS gaps are reported only as descriptive context. The two-seed-per-instance table is treated as a development evaluation; no claim of universal dominance or statistical significance is made.

\subsection{Active-Learning Development Evaluation}
Table~\ref{tab:active} and Fig.~\ref{fig:active-comparison} report the two-seed-per-instance development evaluation. Each row averages two independent runs with the same candidate-pool size, calibration size, final $K$, and five-second MIP limit. AL-RF has the lowest or tied-lowest mean in six of ten instances and the lowest overall mean across the 20 instance--seed runs. Its mean best feasible makespan is 211.7, compared with 214.2 for RF-H, 214.6 for LB, 212.8 for HeurSeq, and 217.9 for Random. The gain is largest on MK03, MK06, MK08, and MK10; MK09 remains a difficult case where the direct heuristic or RF-H can be better. These results motivate the active calibration mechanism while preserving a transparent failure case.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What Did the Surrogate Learn?}
The initial model receives workload, assignment-usage, operation-count, imbalance, dispersion, and maximum job-time summaries. AL-RF adds a small set of MIP-verified labels near the heuristic frontier. Training, calibration, and test assignments are generated from disjoint pools, and the relevant evidence is the common-pool Top-$K$ outcome and solver status rather than in-sample fit.

\subsection{When Is Learned Guidance Better Than Simple Scoring?}
AL-RF improves the average shortlist quality in the five-second development evaluation, but the instance-level variation shows why the lower-bound and direct heuristic controls remain useful. The practical recommendation is to use active calibration when a small extra MIP budget is available and to retain RF-H, LB, and HeurSeq as inexpensive controls when that budget is unavailable.

\subsection{Why Does the Method Fail on Certain Instances?}
The evaluation exposes two measurable failure modes: time-limited MIP verification becomes inconclusive on larger instances, and active calibration can spend substantial time without improving a particular instance. These observations are protocol-level limitations rather than causal explanations of precedence conflicts or feature sparsity.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
The workflow is useful when a scheduler needs an auditable shortlist of machine assignments and can reserve a fixed solver budget for final checks. Its feature vector is easy to inspect, and its candidate-level status and gap records expose when the shortlist is not certified. Deployment should keep a simple lower-bound or heuristic control and should not interpret a time-limited incumbent as an optimal production plan.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations remain. The feature set is intentionally low-dimensional and omits explicit precedence interactions. Large instances can still return feasible incumbents with nonzero gaps or no incumbent within the five-second budget. Active calibration adds solver calls and its benefit varies by instance; the current evaluation uses two seeds per instance and one benchmark family. Cross-benchmark transfer, larger calibration pools, longer budgets, and comparisons with established metaheuristics remain future work.

% ═══════════════════════════ 7. CONCLUSION ═══════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}

We have presented an active-learning-assisted candidate-screening workflow for Flexible Job Shop Scheduling. A small set of MIP-verified assignments corrects heuristic labels before a Random Forest ranks a common test pool, and every final shortlist receives the same verification budget. Across Brandimarte MK01--MK10 with two seeds per instance, AL-RF achieves the best overall mean feasible incumbent among the tested screeners and improves over the original RF-H, lower-bound, direct heuristic, and random controls. The candidate-level release makes calibration cost, feasibility, and solver gaps visible for reproduction.

The method is designed as a decision-support component for intelligent manufacturing. Its strongest use case is a scheduler with a small calibration budget and a strict final verification budget; when calibration time is unavailable, the simpler controls remain appropriate.
